# Negligence — Duty, Breach, Causation and Damage

## Introduction

Negligence in tort concerns the breach of a legally recognised duty of care causing actionable harm. It is not sufficient merely to show that someone behaved carelessly: the law must recognise a duty to the claimant, its breach, a causal connection and recoverable harm.

## Elements for a long exam answer

1. **Duty of care:** Did the defendant owe a duty to this claimant in the circumstances? Explain foreseeability, proximity and relevant limits.
2. **Breach of duty:** Did the defendant fall below the applicable standard of reasonable care? The level and circumstances of risk matter.
3. **Causation:** Did the breach cause the harm? Distinguish factual causation from legal causation.
4. **Damage and remoteness:** Was the damage actionable and within the scope of liability? Discuss remoteness separately where relevant.
5. **Defences and qualifications:** Address contributory negligence, volenti non fit injuria and any statutory context only where applicable.

## Landmark comparative authority

[[Donoghue v Stevenson - 1932]] **[1932] AC 562**, House of Lords: the manufacturer/consumer setting and Lord Atkin's neighbour principle illustrate that a duty may arise even without a contractual relationship. This is an **English** authority, not a binding decision of an Indian court.

## Illustration

A shop operator leaves an unmarked spill in a walkway. A customer slips and is injured. Analyse whether the operator owed a duty, failed to take reasonable precautions, caused the injury, and whether the damage was foreseeable. Consider any factual role of the customer's own conduct.

## Short-answer revision

Negligence requires **duty → breach → causation → damage** (subject to legal qualifications). Explain each with the facts, then consider defences and remoteness.
